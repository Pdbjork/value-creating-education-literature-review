Educational thought and practice

Josei Toda’s Contributions to Education

Teacher, methods writer, publisher, organizer, and transmitter of value-creating education

Prepared by Peter Bjork, M.Ed., DePaul University - College of Education - PhD in Value-Creating Education for Global Citizens

Summary: Josei Toda (1900–1958) did not originate Makiguchi’s value-creating pedagogy, and he did not establish today’s Soka schools. His distinctive contribution was to turn an emerging theory into teachable methods, practical publications, an organized movement, and a postwar educational ethos linking personal transformation with peace.

Best description: Toda was less an independent academic theorist than an implementer, editor, popularizer, and institutional bridge—from Tsunesaburō Makiguchi’s educational work to the later Soka tradition developed under Daisaku Ikeda.

Seven principal contributions

Contribution 1

Teaching directed toward struggling learners

Toda began teaching in a Hokkaido mining community and later worked under Makiguchi in Tokyo. His recurring problem was practical: how to help children labeled low-achieving become capable, independent learners.

Contribution 2

Jishu Gakkan as a site of practice

In 1923 he opened a private tutorial school preparing pupils for competitive secondary-school entrance examinations. It gave him room to adapt pacing, diagnose difficulties, and test methods beyond ordinary classroom constraints.

Contribution 3

Reasoning-centered instructional materials

His 1930 arithmetic guide organized problems around comparison, classification, and inference. The aim was not memorizing answer forms but acquiring a habit of reasoning. It reportedly sold about one million copies.

Contribution 4

Broad educational publishing

Toda wrote or edited materials on home education, reading, arithmetic, science, geography, and history. He also produced teacher periodicals connecting principles to usable lessons and teaching materials.

Contribution 5

Making Makiguchi’s system publishable

He helped finance, organize, edit, and promote the early volumes of The System of Value-Creating Pedagogy. Makiguchi credited Toda’s commitment with moving the long-delayed project into print.

Contribution 6

Organization and transmission

Toda supported a value-creating pedagogy group and educational journals, then later transmitted Makiguchi’s educational aspiration to Ikeda. His role preserved continuity across war, imprisonment, and postwar reconstruction.

Contribution 7

Connecting education, human transformation, and peace

As postwar Soka Gakkai president, Toda framed “human revolution” as change in the individual that could alter society. His 1957 declaration against nuclear weapons gave the later Soka educational tradition a durable peace-and-citizenship orientation.

From method to movement

What Toda Added in Practice

1. A pedagogy of reasoning, not answer imitation

Toda’s clearest original work in education was instructional rather than metaphysical. In arithmetic, he emphasized recognizing similarities and differences among problems, inferring relationships, and deciding how a problem should be approached. This translated Makiguchi’s broad commitment to value creation into a concrete learning process.

“It is not a learning in which we teach the way to solve a question or in which a student simply commits an answer to memory. Rather, it is a learning to enable the student to think how to solve a question.”

Josei Toda, quoted by Masayuki Shiohara from the 1930 arithmetic guide

Diagnosis before uniform pacing

Toda attributed some low attainment to uniform instruction that ignored differences in learners’ pace and preparation. The practical response was targeted explanation and adjusted sequencing—not lowered expectations.

Ability beyond memorization

His writing on home education named observation, presentation, understanding, memory, reasoning, decision, and will. Entrance-exam success mattered, but it was not supposed to exhaust the purpose of learning.

Teachers as makers of usable knowledge

By writing subject guides and editing teaching journals, Toda treated educators as investigators who should classify difficulties, preserve successful methods, and circulate classroom knowledge.

Making theory operational

Makiguchi supplied the larger account of happiness and value. Toda supplied materials, publishing work, organizational energy, and a setting in which instructional claims could be tried.

Working within a competitive system

Jishu Gakkan prepared pupils for selective entrance examinations. Toda sought to humanize and make effective a system he also criticized. This is both a practical strength and an unresolved tension.

Mentorship as educational transmission

Toda’s long-term influence came through people as much as texts. His mentorship of Ikeda carried Makiguchi’s educational aspiration into institutions established after Toda’s death.

2. Editorial and institutional work

Toda’s contribution to The System of Value-Creating Pedagogy was substantial: organizing accumulated notes, preparing manuscripts, helping with publication, building support, and promoting the books through educational networks. Later Soka tradition also credits him with proposing the compressed term sōka, from “value creation.”

Evidence qualification

The familiar story in which Toda coins sōka during a 1930 conversation comes from a narrative he published in 1952, not a surviving contemporaneous transcript. It is reasonable to report it as institutional tradition, not as independently verified dialogue.

3. A bridge, not the founder of the later school system

Toda preserved and transmitted the aspiration to establish schools based on value-creating education, but the modern Soka schools and Soka University were founded later under Daisaku Ikeda. Toda’s contribution was vision, succession, and continuity—not direct administration of institutions that did not yet exist.

Legacy and assessment

Peace Education, Limits, and Lasting Value

The 1957 nuclear-weapons declaration

Toda asked younger members to attack the logic of nuclear weapons “at its root,” grounding his appeal in a universal right to live rather than allegiance to a particular state. This became a foundational reference for later Soka work in peace education, disarmament advocacy, exhibitions, testimony, and research.

Read the rhetoric historically and completely

The declaration also demanded the death penalty for anyone who used nuclear weapons. That language is in tension with a contemporary human-rights framework and should not be omitted. Later interpreters generally emphasize Toda’s underlying target: the dehumanizing will that treats mass annihilation as legitimate.

Classroom implications of Toda’s legacy

  • Teach the structure of reasoning: students should compare, classify, infer, explain, and choose methods.
  • Diagnose individual obstacles: do not confuse a mismatch in pacing or explanation with incapacity.
  • Use examinations without letting them define education: preparation can build capability, but scores are not the final aim.
  • Turn practice into shared professional knowledge: teachers should document and circulate effective methods.
  • Connect learning with agency: knowledge should increase a learner’s ability to transform circumstances responsibly.
  • Make peace education confront moral logic: study not only weapons and treaties but the ideas that permit dehumanization.

Limits of the contribution

  • Toda did not produce a systematic educational philosophy comparable in scope to Makiguchi’s.
  • His strongest work was practical, editorial, and organizational.
  • His tutorial school combined humanistic aims with highly competitive examination preparation.
  • Much biographical evidence comes from Soka-affiliated archives and later recollection.
  • His postwar educational language became inseparable from Nichiren Buddhist practice and Soka Gakkai expansion.
  • His broad influence is greatest inside the Soka tradition, not mainstream instructional research.

Bottom line: Toda’s importance lies in implementation and continuity. He made value-creating education teachable, publishable, organizable, and transmissible—then connected it to a postwar ethic of personal transformation and peace. His legacy is strongest when described as practical and institutional rather than credited with originating Makiguchi’s theory or founding institutions created after his death.
