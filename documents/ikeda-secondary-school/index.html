Design invariants

  • Every learner is known. An advisor tracks strengths, concerns and growth.
  • High care + high challenge. Belief in potential never means lowering standards.
  • Knowledge has a destination. Lessons ask what learning enables us to understand, create or repair.
  • Difference is educative. Disagreement is examined, not feared or forced into consensus.
  • The local community is a text. Nearby realities open onto global interdependence.
  • Beauty matters. Literature, art, music, craft and well-made work cultivate sensibility.

The lesson

A 60-minute value-creating lesson

Dialogue does not replace subject teaching. The cycle moves from human connection to explicit disciplinary knowledge, inquiry, encounter with difference, useful action and reflective self-revision.

1Connect0–4 min

Greet, notice, recall. Establish emotional and intellectual presence.

2Question4–9

Frame a consequential problem with more than one plausible view.

3Teach9–21

Model the concept, method or disciplinary tool novices need.

4Inquire21–37

Teams analyze, calculate, interpret or build—with accountable roles.

5Dialogue37–47

Test claims across perspectives; listen for what could change one’s view.

6Create value47–56

Apply knowledge to a decision, explanation, design or contribution.

7Reflect56–60

Show individual learning; name revision and next responsibility.

PROPOSALTime is a design constraint, not doctrine. Shorter or longer blocks can preserve the same intellectual arc.

Worked example · mathematics + environmental science

Driving question: Can our school cut lunchtime food waste by 40% without reducing nutrition or shifting cost unfairly?
Learning: percentage change, sampling error, proportional reasoning, material flows, stakeholder trade-offs. Product: a quantified 1-page proposal for the cafeteria team.
TimeTeacher moveStudent evidence
0–4ConnectPrivately estimate yesterday’s waste from a photograph; pair-check assumptions.
4–9QuestionIdentify who is affected: diners, staff, growers, budget holders, landfill neighbors.
9–21Teach preciselyFollow a worked example; distinguish 40 percentage points from a 40% reduction; critique a biased sample.
21–37Coach inquiryCalculate a baseline and confidence limits from a supplied 10-day dataset; compare two interventions.
37–47Structure dialogueUse stakeholder roles; make a claim, cite data, restate a contrary view fairly, then respond.
47–56Demand usefulnessSelect one action, quantify expected impact and cost, state one risk and a 2-week test.
56–60Make learning visibleExit slip: one corrected calculation, one view changed by evidence, one unresolved question.

Dialogue protocol

  1. Claim: “Our evidence suggests…”
  2. Listen: “I hear your concern is…”
  3. Test: “What would count against our view?”
  4. Bridge: “We agree on…; we differ on…”
  5. Revise: “I now think… because…”

No forced consensus. Courage includes respectful disagreement; wisdom includes changing one’s mind.

Success criteria

  • Mathematics is accurate and units are clear.
  • Evidence and uncertainty are distinguished.
  • A competing view is represented fairly.
  • The proposal creates plausible personal and social benefit.
  • The student can name what they revised.

Teacher’s closing question

“What can this knowledge now help us do wisely—here, with the people actually affected?”

The teaching strategy

High challenge, high care, shared growth

IKEDAThe humanity of the teacher is central, and trust begins with the teacher’s belief in students. PRACTICETokyo Soka High School names human capacity, dialogue, intellect and social capacity among its aims. [2, 5]

1Know the learner; earn trust

Use names, interests, work history and regular 1:1 check-ins. Encourage specifically. Maintain professional boundaries; never make care performative.

2Teach the discipline explicitly

State the concept, model expert thinking, check understanding, then release responsibility. Humanism is not intellectual vagueness.

3Design real dialogue

Use rich questions, preparation time, evidence, turn structures and metacognitive reflection. Invite dissent; intervene when dignity or facts are attacked.

4Make cooperation accountable

Assign roles that rotate—facilitator, skeptic, analyst, connector. Require both a shared product and individual evidence of understanding.

5Link local and global

Begin with a nearby system, person or place; trace its connections to human rights, peace, environment and development. Return to feasible local action.

6Normalize revision

Give feedback on the next move. Preserve drafts and reflection. Praise courage, accuracy and improvement—not compliance or a fixed “good student” identity.

PROPOSALAssessment through four lenses

Masteryaccurate knowledge, technique, vocabulary
Reasoningevidence, inference, uncertainty, critique
Dialoguelistening, fair representation, constructive response
Applicationusefulness, consequence, revision, transfer

Guardrail: grade observable work—not beliefs, personality, agreeableness, religious identity or extroversion. Subject mastery remains non-negotiable; schools set weights by course and task.

Plan every lesson with 6 questions

  1. What exact disciplinary knowledge must students gain?
  2. Why could this matter to human life or the world?
  3. What will I explain or model directly?
  4. Where will students encounter a meaningful difference?
  5. What will they create, decide, explain or improve?
  6. What individual evidence will prove learning?

Restorative discipline with boundaries

Dignity forbids humiliation; it does not forbid consequences. Protect safety first, establish facts, hear impact, require repair and support a return to learning.

The teacher’s day-to-day craft

  • Greet students at the threshold.
  • Scan who is silent, absent or thriving.
  • Teach one idea with exceptional clarity.
  • Ask a question whose answer is not staged.
  • Confer with one learner about their work.
  • Record one actionable observation.
  • Exchange evidence with a colleague.
  • Reflect: “What did students teach me?”

Mutual growth does not erase adult responsibility: the teacher still protects standards, time, truth and safety.

The people system

  • Advisory: 12–16 students; one adult stays with the group for multiple years where possible.
  • Protected teacher learning: 2 common planning periods weekly; examine student work, not impressions.
  • Family connection: communicate strengths before problems; invite knowledge and language from home.
  • Community partners: co-design questions and receive useful products; service is reciprocal, not charity theatre.
  • Student voice: real input on routines, projects and climate—within transparent adult responsibility.

Monthly / termly pulse

Monthly: public dialogue or arts event; local fieldwork; advisor review of belonging and progress.

Each term: exhibition of rigorous work; student-led reflection; community partner feedback; faculty study of who is flourishing and who is not.

A weekly learning rhythm

MON · PURPOSELaunch consequential questions; diagnose knowledge; locate the human stakes.
TUE · KNOWLEDGEBuild disciplinary concepts, vocabulary and techniques through explicit teaching.
WED · INQUIRYInvestigate, calculate, read, experiment and critique evidence with others.
THU · APPLICATIONFieldwork, design, cross-cultural encounter or problem-solving with a real audience.
FRI · CONTRIBUTIONShare, receive critique, revise, reflect and decide the next responsibility.

Guardrails against distortion

No doctrinal teaching, worship or personality cult.
No forced harmony or suppression of dissent.
No “happiness” used to deny grief or struggle.
No dialogue used as a substitute for evidence.
No care without boundaries or safeguarding.
No service that exploits students or communities.
